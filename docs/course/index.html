Ask What Matters
01

"How was your stay?"

Why the most common survey question is also the most useless — and what to ask instead.

A guest checks out of a hotel

They open the survey on their phone. It asks: "How was your stay? Any comments?" They type "fine, thanks" and tap submit.

The hotel learns nothing. The guest moves on. Multiply this by five million stays and you've got a lake of lukewarm data that can't tell you what's actually broken.

⚠️
The open-ended box problem

Generic prompts produce generic answers. Guests don't know what the hotel wants to hear, so they default to polite nothings. The hotel gets stars and silence.

This project's answer: ask better follow-ups

This is Ask What Matters, submitted to the Wharton Hack-AI-thon 2026. After a guest submits their review and ratings, a small agent reads what they wrote and picks exactly two targeted follow-up questions.

Not random questions. Not the same two for every guest. Two questions chosen because this guest, at this hotel, is the right person to answer them right now.

🎯

Context-aware

The agent reads the guest's actual review, their star ratings, and the hotel's history before picking anything.

📐

Rule-driven

Hard rules decide what to ask. An AI only polishes the wording. Fewer hallucinations, more predictability.

🔄

Self-improving

Every answer updates the hotel's profile, so the next guest gets questions about genuinely unknown things.

What Priya actually sees

Two question cards slide in. She taps options instead of writing essays — low effort, high information.

1
"We're sorry the room smelled musty. Were there any other areas that could be improved?"

Options: Apart from that, everything else was fine · Staff & service · Noise · Food · Other

2
"A past guest reported no fridges in the rooms. Was there a fridge in your room?"

Options: Yes, worked fine · No, there wasn't one · Yes but broken · Didn't check

💡
Why two, not twenty?

Every extra question bleeds response rates. The whole design is ruthlessly triaged down to two because that's the sweet spot between "learn something new" and "guest taps close."

Meet the Cast

Six characters do all the work. Once you know who they are, the whole system makes sense.

Think of it as a small theatre company

A play isn't one actor on stage — it's a cast, each with one job. Costume, lighting, script, director. You don't need to understand every rope backstage if you know who plays what role.

Our app has six roles. Everything the system does is one of these six characters doing their one thing.

The big idea: separation of concerns

Notice that no single actor "does everything." The Agent doesn't read regex. The Rules don't write sentences. The LLM doesn't touch the data files. Each character does one thing and hands off.

💡
Separation of concerns, in plain English

When each piece has a narrow job, a bug is easier to trace ("which actor is lying?"), a change is easier to make ("just swap the LLM"), and a test is easier to write ("does the Rule Engine detect 'musty'? ignore everything else"). This is one of the oldest and most important patterns in software.

03

The Two-Slot Decision

One fixed question, one situational question. That single rule pins down everything the agent does.

A journalist's two questions

Imagine a reporter interviewing someone about a city council meeting. They have time for exactly two questions. What do they ask?

Question one is obvious: "You mentioned you were frustrated about the zoning vote — can you say more?" You follow up on what the source just said.

Question two depends on context. If a previous source contradicted them, the reporter asks them to settle it. If nobody's mentioned a specific angle yet, the reporter probes that blank spot.

💡
This agent does exactly that

Slot 1 is always the "you just told me X" follow-up. Slot 2 is either conflict resolution (when the hotel has unsettled disagreements) or information gap (when a topic is suspiciously uncovered).

The two slots, visualized

1

Slot 1 — Comment Deep-Dive

Always about this guest's review and ratings. Acknowledge what they said, then dig deeper or broaden. Never robotic.

2a

Slot 2a — Conflict Resolution

If past reviews disagree ("pool was great" vs "pool was closed"), ask the current guest to be the tiebreaker.

2b

Slot 2b — Information Gap

If no conflicts exist, pick a topic the hotel has zero data on (breakfast? accessibility?) and ask about that.

Critical constraint: Slot 2 is either conflict or gap. Never both. Never neither. Conflicts always win when they exist.

The decision in code

The whole "conflict beats gap" logic lives in one six-line block inside [backend/agent.py](backend/agent.py). Plain code, no AI needed.

CODE 
# Check for unresolved conflicts on this property
conflicts: list[dict] = []
if resolved_pid:
    conflicts = get_active_conflicts(resolved_pid, top_n=1)

# Slot 2: conflict takes priority; only load gaps when no conflicts
gap_aspects: list[dict] = []
if not conflicts:
    gap_aspects = _get_gap_aspects(resolved_pid or "", review_mentioned)
PLAIN ENGLISH

Start with an empty list of conflicts.

If we know which hotel this is, look up its conflicts file and grab the top one (if any).

Now Slot 2's decision: if there's any conflict, we'll ask about that. Only if there are zero conflicts do we bother computing information gaps.

This if not conflicts is the entire priority rule — four words doing all the work.

The decision tree, end-to-end

1
Read the review text

Run regex over it to detect which topics the guest already mentioned. Those get marked as "already covered."

2
Look up the hotel's conflict list

Does this property have any unresolved "guests disagree" topics? If yes, pick the top one for Slot 2.

3
No conflicts? Compute information gaps

Score every topic by how little this hotel has been reviewed on it. Skip topics the guest already covered.

4
Build one big prompt

Hand Slot 1 context (the review, ratings) and Slot 2 context (either the conflict snippets or the gap list) to the LLM.

5
Get JSON back, render

The LLM returns two questions in a strict JSON schema. The frontend turns them into tap-able cards.

How the "information gap" gets picked

The gap logic has a clever trick: it splits candidate topics into tiers by coverage, then randomly picks within tiers. So never-mentioned topics come first, but different guests aren't always asked the same thing.

CODE 
tier_a: list[dict] = []  # <= 5% coverage
tier_b: list[dict] = []  # 5-25% coverage

for aspect_key, info in aspect_dict.items():
    if aspect_key in mentioned_set: continue
    # ... compute pct ...
    if pct <= 5.0: tier_a.append(entry)
    elif pct <= 25.0: tier_b.append(entry)

random.shuffle(tier_a); random.shuffle(tier_b)
pool = tier_a + tier_b
return pool[:pick_n]
PLAIN ENGLISH

Two buckets: "barely any reviews mention this" and "some reviews mention this."

For each possible topic, skip any the guest already wrote about.

If this hotel has less than 5% coverage on the topic, it goes into the urgent bucket. Between 5% and 25% goes into the less-urgent bucket.

Shuffle within each bucket so different guests see different questions — but the urgent bucket always gets picked from first.

Return the top three. The LLM picks one of these for Slot 2b.

🔍
Why tiers, not pure scoring?

A pure score would give the same top-3 to every guest forever, creating a feedback loop. Tiers + randomness spread the data-gathering effort across many topics while still prioritizing the biggest blanks. Crowdsourcing in disguise.

04

Rules Pick. LLM Polishes.

The hybrid pattern that keeps the system testable and gives it human warmth.

Think of a talk show host

Before the show, a producer writes cue cards: "Ask about her new album. Mention the Grammy. Pivot to the tour dates." The cards are blunt and structural. The host never reads them aloud.

On stage, the host turns those cards into a flowing conversation — adding warmth, timing jokes, rephrasing for the moment. The producer decides the topic. The host picks the words.

💡
That's exactly what this agent does

Python rules are the producer. They write structured cue cards: "Slot 1: acknowledge the bathroom complaint. Slot 2: resolve the fridge conflict. Use these exact 4 options." The LLM is the host. It takes that structure and writes natural, polite questions.

Why not just let the LLM decide everything?

"Just dump the review into ChatGPT and ask for two questions" would save a lot of code. It would also introduce problems the team chose to avoid.

🎲

Non-determinism

Same review, different questions each call. Impossible to test. The gap-priority logic couldn't be proven with a unit test.

👻

Hallucinations

The LLM might invent a conflict ("past guests mentioned bedbugs") that doesn't exist in the data. Rules can only reference real conflicts.

💉

Prompt injection

A malicious guest could write "ignore previous instructions" in their review. With rules, the LLM only controls wording — it can't change the system's behavior.

🎯

Predictable priorities

Bathroom issues score 8.84; location scores 1.80. Rules encode what matters. LLMs have no way to know which gaps the hotel actually cares about.

Step 1 — rules read the review

Before any AI is involved, regex patterns scan the review for topics. Nothing fancy. Just matching words.

CODE 
def detect_aspects(text: str) -> set[Aspect]:
    """Regex-based detection: fast, cheap, testable."""
    if not text: return set()
    low = text.lower()
    hits: set[Aspect] = set()
    for aspect, patterns in ASPECT_PATTERNS.items():
        for pat in patterns:
            if re.search(pat, low):
                hits.add(aspect)
                break
    return hits
PLAIN ENGLISH

Take the review text and lowercase it so "Bathroom" matches "bathroom."

Walk through every known topic (bathroom, smell, noise, staff, ...) and its keyword patterns.

As soon as any pattern matches, mark that topic as mentioned and stop checking its other patterns. One hit is enough.

Hand back the set of topics this review covers. The agent uses this to avoid asking about things the guest already told us.

Step 2 — rules hand the LLM a cue card

The agent builds a long, structured prompt. It's not a polite request — it's a specification. It names the slots, the allowed options, the tone rules, and the exact JSON shape to return.

CODE (excerpt) 
QUESTION 1 — COMMENT DEEP-DIVE  (role: "comment_deepdive")

Case B: SPECIFIC negative:
  Step 1 — Acknowledge with empathy: "We're sorry to hear..."
  Step 2 — Ask about OTHER areas: "Were there any other aspects..."
  Options should be BROAD, covering different aspects EXCLUDING
  the one they already complained about:
    - FIRST option MUST be a positive escape:
      "Apart from that, everything else was great"
    - Then list 3-5 other aspects
PLAIN ENGLISH

The prompt is literally a script. It spells out: "Here's the exact template for this case."

For a specific complaint (like "mold in bathroom"), the rules require two sentence structures: apology, then a broadening question.

Even the options list is prescribed — the first option has to be an emotional release ("Apart from that, everything else was great") so the guest isn't forced to keep complaining.

The LLM only has freedom in how to word each piece. The shape and intent are fixed by the rules.

Step 3 — the output is locked into a shape

Rather than trusting the LLM to return valid JSON, the agent uses OpenAI's JSON schema feature: "Return exactly this structure, with these allowed values, or the call fails."

CODE (excerpt) 
{
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
        "qid": {"type": "string"},
        "role": {"type": "string", "enum": ALLOWED_ROLES},
        "aspect": {"type": "string", "enum": ALLOWED_ASPECTS},
        "text_en": {"type": "string"},
        "response_type": {
            "type": "string",
            "enum": ALLOWED_RESPONSE_TYPES,
        },
        "options": {"type": "array", "items": {"type": "string"}},
        "private": {"type": "boolean"},
        "reason": {"type": "string"},
    },
}
PLAIN ENGLISH

Each question must be a JSON object with these fields — no more, no less.

Role has to be one of a fixed list (comment_deepdive, conflict_resolution, information_gap). The LLM literally cannot invent a new role.

Aspect is locked to the 18 known topics. If the LLM tries to output "banana_bread_quality" the API rejects the response.

response_type is one of four shapes the frontend knows how to render (quick_tap, multi_select, free_text, private_text). No surprises.

Every field is required — no missing keys, no hallucinated keys. The downstream code can trust what it gets.

05

The Data Flywheel

Every guest's answer makes the next guest's questions smarter. Here's the compounding loop.

Think of Waze on your commute

When you drive with Waze open, your phone reports your speed. Thousands of other drivers do the same. That's how Waze knows traffic is slow on a bridge you haven't reached yet.

Each driver is both a consumer (they get a route) and a contributor (they leak data about their own trip). The system gets smarter for the next person because of the last one.

💡
This app does the same thing with reviews

Every time a guest answers a follow-up, the hotel's evidence profile updates — what topics are now covered, which ones are still dark. The next guest walks into a smarter agent because of the last one's taps.

Three files carry the memory

📊
hotel_evidence_profiles.json — coverage map

Per hotel: what topics have been mentioned, how often, and which have never been mentioned. Updates after every follow-up submission.

⚡
hotel_conflicts.json — disagreement log

Pre-computed list of topics where past reviews contradict. "Staff was rude" in March vs "staff was wonderful" in June. Built offline, read on every request.

🏨
property_intel.json — what the hotel claims

Parsed from hotel descriptions: "has pool, spa, breakfast, parking, wifi." If a hotel claims a pool but no guest has validated it yet, that's a high-priority gap.

Priya's taps become tomorrow's data

When Priya submits her two answers, the frontend posts them to [api/submit-followups.py](api/submit-followups.py). That endpoint calls one function, and the hotel's profile changes forever.

1
Frontend sends answers

A JSON body with the property ID, the two questions that were asked, and Priya's tapped options.

2
Backend matches answers to evidence labels

"There was no fridge" → matches the room_amenities label. "Staff & service" → matches staff label.

3
The coverage percentages update

Those labels' counts go up. Their percentages re-compute. The never_mentioned list gets shorter.

4
The cache is invalidated

The agent's in-memory copy of profiles gets cleared so the next API call reads fresh data. This is how the flywheel turns.

The clever trick: local sentiment

Detecting conflicts is harder than it sounds. A review might say "Room was perfect, but the bathroom had mold." Is that positive or negative? Depends on which topic you're asking about.

The conflict detector looks at a small window around each mention and scores sentiment just within that window. Not the whole review.

CODE 
def _snippet_sentiment(text: str, match_start: int, match_end: int, window: int = 80) -> str:
    """Local window sentiment around a topic mention."""
    lo = max(0, match_start - window)
    hi = min(len(text), match_end + window)
    snippet = text[lo:hi].lower()

    pos = sum(1 for w in POSITIVE_CUES if w in snippet)
    neg = sum(1 for w in NEGATIVE_CUES if w in snippet)

    return "neg" if neg > pos else ("pos" if pos > neg else "neutral")
PLAIN ENGLISH

When the text mentions a topic (say, "bathroom"), grab the 80 characters before and after that word. That's our evidence window.

Count how many positive words ("clean", "perfect", "loved") and negative words ("dirty", "mold", "broken") appear in just that window.

Whichever side has more wins — the topic is marked positive, negative, or neutral for that specific mention.

A review that's 90% glowing but mentions "mold in the bathroom" will correctly mark the bathroom as negative, even though the overall review is positive.

🔍
Why this matters for conflicts

Conflicts are detected by comparing these local sentiments across different reviews. Positive bathroom in one review + negative bathroom in another = a conflict the agent can surface to the next guest.

Why this pattern is powerful

A static question bank asks the same things forever. A pure-LLM system forgets everything between requests. This design remembers — and uses its memory to ask better questions each cycle.

💡
Three small patterns, one big lesson

Read-through cache + cache invalidation + file-based persistence gives you a system that's simple enough to debug and smart enough to improve. No database, no ML training, no complicated infrastructure. Just plain JSON files and careful code.

That's the whole system

Six actors. Two question slots. One priority rule (conflicts beat gaps). One design philosophy (rules pick, LLM polishes). One data flywheel that compounds knowledge per submission.

You now have the mental model to read any file in the repo and know roughly why it's there. The rest is just detail.
